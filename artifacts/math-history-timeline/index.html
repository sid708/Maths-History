The Infinite Journey

From scratched bones to the edges of infinity

Mathematics did not begin with a single discovery. It grew from the human need to count, measure, predict the seasons, divide land, and understand the patterns of the sky. ~70,000 – 20,000 BCE

The Dawn of Pattern

In the Blombos Cave of South Africa, ochre rocks were engraved with geometric cross-hatch patterns. These are among the earliest known deliberate geometric designs created by humans — evidence that abstract pattern recognition existed tens of thousands of years before writing.

Later, the Lebombo bone (Swaziland, ~35,000 BCE) and the famous Ishango bone (Congo, ~20,000 BCE) show rows of notches. Scholars debate whether these represent lunar calendars, prime numbers, or early multiplication systems. Whatever their exact purpose, they prove that humans were already quantifying the world.

Counting Geometry
c. 3400 – 1600 BCE

Birth of Number Systems

The Sumerians of Mesopotamia invented the first true numeral system and a sophisticated system of weights and measures. By ~2400 BCE they had developed a sexagesimal (base-60) place-value system — the reason we still divide hours into 60 minutes and circles into 360 degrees.

In Egypt, hieroglyphic numerals appeared around 3100 BCE. The Rhind Papyrus (c. 1650 BCE) and the Moscow Mathematical Papyrus (c. 1850 BCE) contain problems of arithmetic, geometry, and even early algebra. Egyptian scribes calculated areas of triangles, volumes of pyramids, and approximated π as 256/81 ≈ 3.1605.

Egyptian π ≈ 3.1605 (256/81)

The famous tablet Plimpton 322 (c. 1800 BCE) lists Pythagorean triples centuries before Pythagoras, showing advanced understanding of right triangles.

c. 600 – 400 BCE

Thales, Pythagoras & the Birth of Proof

Thales of Miletus (c. 624–546 BCE) is often called the first true mathematician. He is credited with proving geometric theorems deductively rather than by measurement alone — that a circle is bisected by its diameter, that angles in a semicircle are right angles, and that corresponding angles are equal when a transversal crosses parallel lines.

Pythagoras of Samos (c. 570–495 BCE) and his followers elevated number to a mystical principle. The Pythagorean theorem was known earlier, but the school proved it rigorously and discovered that √2 is irrational — a scandal that shattered their belief that all is number (rational number).

a² + b² = c²

Zeno of Elea’s paradoxes (Achilles and the tortoise, the arrow) forced deep thinking about infinity, continuity, and motion — problems that would only be resolved with the invention of calculus two thousand years later.

Proof Irrational numbers
c. 300 BCE

Euclid’s Elements — The Temple of Geometry

Working in Alexandria under the Ptolemies, Euclid compiled and systematized virtually all existing Greek geometric knowledge into the thirteen books of the Elements. Beginning from a handful of definitions, postulates, and common notions, he deduced hundreds of theorems with a rigor that remained the gold standard for more than two millennia.

He proved the infinitude of primes, presented the Euclidean algorithm for greatest common divisors, and constructed the five Platonic solids. The parallel postulate would later become the seed of non-Euclidean geometries.

There are infinitely many prime numbers.

The Elements was the most successful textbook in history, remaining in continuous use until the 19th century. Its axiomatic method still underpins modern mathematics.

Axiomatic method Geometry Number theory
c. 287 – 212 BCE

Archimedes — Master of π and Exhaustion

Archimedes of Syracuse is widely regarded as the greatest mathematician of antiquity. Using the method of exhaustion (a precursor of integral calculus), he proved that the area of a circle is πr² and that the surface area and volume of a sphere are 4πr² and (4/3)πr³ respectively.

By inscribing and circumscribing regular polygons with 96 sides, he rigorously established:

3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7
≈ 3.1408 < π < 3.1429

He also invented the Archimedean spiral, calculated areas under parabolas, and developed a system for expressing enormous numbers (in The Sand-Reckoner). His work on levers, buoyancy, and centers of gravity founded mathematical physics.

π Method of exhaustion Proto-calculus
3rd – 1st century BCE

Eratosthenes, Apollonius & Hipparchus

Eratosthenes (c. 276–194 BCE) invented the sieve for finding prime numbers and measured the circumference of the Earth with remarkable accuracy using geometry and the angle of the sun at Syene and Alexandria.

Apollonius of Perga wrote the definitive work on conic sections — ellipse, parabola, hyperbola — introducing the names we still use and studying their properties with extraordinary sophistication.

Hipparchus developed trigonometry (the chord table) and founded scientific astronomy. Later, Ptolemy’s Almagest would systematize this knowledge for the next 1,400 years.

Primes Conics Trigonometry
c. 500 BCE – 700 CE

The Invention of Zero & the Decimal System

Indian mathematics developed a revolutionary idea: a symbol for nothingness that was itself a number — śūnya (zero). Early references appear in the Chandaḥśāstra of Pingala (c. 300–200 BCE), which also discusses binary numbers and combinatorial patterns resembling Pascal’s triangle.

By the 5th–6th centuries CE, the place-value decimal system with zero was fully operational. Aryabhata (476–550 CE) gave an accurate approximation of π (3.1416), calculated the circumference of the Earth, and worked with sine tables. He also proposed that the Earth rotates on its axis.

Aryabhata’s π ≈ 62832/20000 = 3.1416

Brahmagupta (598–668 CE) wrote the first clear rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers, including the formula for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral and solutions to quadratic equations. The Bakhshali manuscript contains the earliest known written zero in India.

The concept of zero as both a placeholder and a number of its own made modern calculation possible. Without it, the decimal system, algebra, and calculus as we know them could not exist.

Zero Decimal system π Negative numbers
c. 200 BCE – 1300 CE

Chinese Mathematics

The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art (compiled by the 1st century CE) is one of the most important ancient mathematical texts. It contains systems of linear equations solved by what is essentially Gaussian elimination, calculations of areas and volumes, and the Chinese remainder theorem.

Liu Hui (3rd century) improved π to 3.1416 using a 192-sided polygon and provided commentaries of remarkable insight. Later, Zu Chongzhi (5th century) calculated π to seven decimal places (355/113), a record that stood for nearly a thousand years.

Zu Chongzhi: 3.1415926 < π < 3.1415927

Chinese mathematicians also developed sophisticated methods for solving higher-degree equations and combinatorial problems.

π Linear algebra
8th – 15th century

The House of Wisdom & Algebra

In Baghdad, the House of Wisdom translated Greek and Indian works and created a new synthesis. Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī (c. 780–850) wrote Kitāb al-jabr wa’l-muqābala — “The Book of Restoration and Balancing” — from which the word algebra derives. He systematized the solution of linear and quadratic equations and introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals to the Islamic world (and eventually Europe).

Al-Khwārizmī’s name also gave us the word algorithm. Later scholars such as Al-Karaji, Omar Khayyam (geometric solutions of cubics), and Al-Kashi (decimal fractions and highly accurate π) pushed the frontiers further.

Trigonometry was transformed into a true mathematical discipline. Spherical trigonometry, the sine, cosine, tangent, and their identities were developed to a high degree. Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) made fundamental contributions to optics and early ideas of infinitesimals.

Algebra Algorithm Trigonometry
1202 – 1500

Fibonacci & the Rebirth of European Mathematics

Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) published Liber Abaci in 1202. It introduced the Hindu-Arabic numeral system (including zero) to Europe, explained commercial arithmetic, and popularized the sequence now named after him:

1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, …

The Fibonacci numbers appear throughout nature and later became central to number theory and computer science. European universities gradually absorbed Greek and Arabic learning through Latin translations. By the late 15th century the first printed editions of Euclid’s Elements appeared (1482).

The Kerala school in India (Madhava, Nilakantha) independently developed infinite series for π, sine, and arctangent — essentially discovering the power series expansions of calculus two centuries before Newton and Leibniz.

Hindu-Arabic numerals Fibonacci Series for π
16th century

Cubics, Quartics & Complex Numbers

Italian mathematicians of the Renaissance cracked the general cubic and quartic equations. Scipione del Ferro, Niccolò Tartaglia, and Gerolamo Cardano published solutions (Cardano’s Ars Magna, 1545). In the process they encountered square roots of negative numbers.

Rafael Bombelli systematized the arithmetic of these “imaginary” quantities. Complex numbers were born — initially viewed with suspicion, later becoming indispensable.

√(−1) = i    (though the symbol came later)

François Viète introduced modern algebraic notation with vowels for unknowns and consonants for knowns, paving the way for symbolic mathematics.

Algebra Complex numbers
1600 – 1687

The Century of Genius — Coordinates, Logs, Probability & Calculus

John Napier invented logarithms (1614), dramatically simplifying calculation. The constant e first appeared in his work.

René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat independently invented analytic geometry, uniting algebra and geometry through coordinates. Descartes’ La Géométrie (1637) is the birth certificate of the Cartesian plane.

Fermat and Blaise Pascal founded the mathematical theory of probability while discussing games of chance. Fermat also stated his Last Theorem in the margin of a book — a conjecture that would wait 357 years for a proof.

Independently, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz invented the calculus. Newton’s fluxions (method of fluxions) and Leibniz’s differentials and the ∫ symbol created the language of continuous change. Newton’s Principia (1687) used these tools to found classical mechanics and universal gravitation.

d/dx (xⁿ) = n xⁿ⁻¹    ∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)

1700 – 1800

Euler — The Master of Us All

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) produced more mathematics than any other individual in history. He standardized modern notation (f(x), e, i, ∑, π as the circle constant), solved the Königsberg bridge problem (inventing graph theory), proved the infinitude of primes via the zeta function, and gave the breathtaking identity:

eiπ + 1 = 0

He worked on almost every branch of mathematics then existing: analysis, number theory, mechanics, optics, music theory, and more. His collected works fill over 70 volumes.

Meanwhile, Johann Lambert proved that π is irrational (1761). The Bernoulli family, Lagrange, Laplace, and many others built the great edifice of 18th-century analysis and mathematical physics.

π irrational Graph theory Analysis
1800 – 1900

Rigor, Non-Euclidean Geometry & Infinity

Carl Friedrich Gauss (“Prince of Mathematicians”) proved the fundamental theorem of algebra, developed differential geometry, number theory (quadratic reciprocity), and non-Euclidean geometry in private. Independently, János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky published hyperbolic geometry, shattering the 2,000-year reign of Euclid’s parallel postulate.

Bernhard Riemann introduced Riemannian geometry — the mathematical language Einstein would later use for general relativity. Georg Cantor created set theory and showed that there are different sizes of infinity: the continuum is strictly larger than the countable infinity of the natural numbers.

ℵ₀ < 2ℵ₀ = |ℝ|

Cauchy, Weierstrass, and Dedekind put analysis on rigorous foundations. Galois invented group theory while solving the insolubility of the quintic. The century ended with Hilbert’s problems and the rise of abstract algebra and topology.

Non-Euclidean Set theory Infinity
1900 – Present

Abstraction, Computers & the Frontiers

The 20th century saw mathematics become vastly more abstract and powerful. Hilbert, Gödel, Turing, and Church founded mathematical logic and computability. Gödel’s incompleteness theorems showed that any sufficiently rich formal system is incomplete. Turing machines defined the limits of mechanical calculation and birthed computer science.

Category theory, algebraic geometry (Grothendieck), differential topology, and the Langlands program reshaped pure mathematics. Computers enabled the proof of the Four Color Theorem (1976) and later the classification of finite simple groups.

Andrew Wiles proved Fermat’s Last Theorem in 1994–95 using elliptic curves and modular forms. Perelman resolved the Poincaré conjecture. Today mathematics underpins cryptography, machine learning, quantum information, and our deepest theories of the physical universe.

From tally marks on bones to the search for a unified theory of mathematics itself, the journey continues — infinite, like the subject it studies.

The Story Never Ends

Mathematics is humanity’s longest continuous intellectual conversation. Every generation inherits the discoveries of the last and adds new chapters. Zero made calculation universal. π connected the circle to the infinite. Calculus gave us the language of change. Set theory and logic revealed the structure of mathematical thought itself.
